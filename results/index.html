    Results¶

    Walk-forward backtest over 2025-04-03 to 2026-11-19, 484 days. The model is refitted from scratch before each day and only ever sees days strictly before the one it is predicting. Every row uses the station reading from 2 days before the day being forecast, which is the freshest the feed now publishes in time. There is no shuffled train/test split anywhere in this project; the rows are a time series and shuffling them would let the model read its own future.

    Pawan 484 9.21 14.48 -0.33 74%
    Persistence (textbook) 459 8.21 12.50 -0.21 78%
    Persistence (operational) 484 10.55 16.22 -0.29 71%
    Climatology 484 18.28 23.10 +3.15 33%
    Raw CAMS 484 41.55 49.61 +38.68 14%

    By season¶

    The targets are set per season because the two are different problems. Calm-season air moves slowly and persistence is hard to beat; burning season is when a forecast is worth having.

    Season Days Pawan MAE Operational persistence MAE Skill Target
    Calm (Dec to Sep) 394 7.98 9.19 +13.2% 10%, met
    Burning (Oct, Nov) 90 14.58 16.48 +11.5% 20%, not met

    On the 74 days whose CAMS forecast was recorded live rather than read back from an archive, Pawan's MAE is 10.47 against 11.09 for operational persistence, +5.6%. No archive has flattered those days, so if this figure and the totals drift apart, this is the one to believe.

    Skill¶

    • Against operational persistence: 12.7% better on MAE.
    • Against textbook persistence: 12.2% worse on MAE.

    The two baselines are both persistence, and the difference between them is the whole argument.

    Operational persistence repeats the freshest reading the CPCB feed had published when the forecast went out, two days old at the reference age. That is the same information Pawan has, so it is a fair comparison, and it is what the forecast is worth to somebody deciding whether to run outside tomorrow.

    Textbook persistence uses yesterday's reading. Nobody has yesterday's reading when the forecast goes out. It is reported because it is the figure quoted in the proposal, and because it marks the ceiling: the gap between the two baselines is the cost of the publication lag, and closing it needs a faster feed rather than a better model.

    How old the reading is¶

    The forecast uses the freshest station reading on file, with a model fitted at exactly that reading's age. The lab scores the shipped model at every age against persistence given the same reading. An older reading is a weaker forecast on its own, so the model's skill over it grows, while the weight the model puts on it shrinks.

    Model comparison¶

    Every candidate is scored on the same 481 days with the same information: the reading the shipped forecast had, and training rows only from days already published. Models that could ship are refitted daily, as the daily job would run them; the scikit-learn benchmarks weekly.     A challenger replaces the shipped model only when it is at least 3 points of skill better in burning season, no more than 2 points worse in calm season, within 5% of the shipped model's error at every reading age from two to five days, and no worse over 20 live days. The first two thresholds were written down in week 7, before any candidate was scored against them. The age test was added when the smoke terms passed at two days and broke down at four.

    Likely range¶

    The likely range is the middle 80% of the backtest's own errors, taken out of sample and per season, so four days in five should land inside it. Sized only from the days before it, the range held the measured value on 365 of 454 backtest days (80%), and on 64 of 90 in burning season (71%), where the first days of a season have the fewest errors to size from.

    Limitations¶

    What the figures above do not show.

    • Burning season is a small sample. 90 burning-season days are scored: 55 read back from archives and 35 recorded live. That is one or two seasons at most, and a dry year, a wet one or a shift in when the stubble is burnt would each move these numbers.
    • Band accuracy only means much in burning season. On calm days almost everything falls in Good, and a method that guessed Good every time would score nearly as well.
    • Archive rows are mildly optimistic. Rows read back from archives take CAMS and weather from series stitched out of the opening hours of successive model runs, which sit a little closer to what happened than a forecast issued a day ahead. The live-only figure, where there is one, is the check on how much that flatters the totals.
    • The feature set was chosen on calm-season data. Every change was made for a stated reason and not only because it scored better, but the reasons and the scores were looked at together. This year's burning season, scored live as it happens, is the test nothing here was tuned against.
    • The outlook's backtest is a little kind. The table keeps one CAMS and weather forecast per day, the day-ahead one, so days two and three are backtested with inputs better than they will have live. Their reading and fire count are moved back properly, which is the larger effect.
