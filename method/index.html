    Method¶

    The problem, precisely¶

    On the morning of day D-1, produce the daily mean PM2.5 at Model Town for day D.

    The word morning is doing real work there. It fixes what the model is allowed to know, and that turns out to be less than the proposal assumed.

    What is actually available at issue time¶

    The daily job is scheduled for 08:00 IST. GitHub starts scheduled jobs late when it is busy, and in practice the run lands in the afternoon, so each forecast records the time it was actually issued. Four backup runs through the day (code/due.py) do the work again only if that run went out without a forecast, or if by evening it has not run at all. They never run ahead of it, so a backup that fires on time cannot forecast from a staler reading. Whenever the job runs, the CAMS and weather forecasts for tomorrow are already out. The station's own readings are a different matter.

    The CPCB feed runs behind, and by how much has changed over the semester. On 17 August the newest published day was 14 August, three days behind. In early October readings arrived in batches every few days. Since late October each day has been published by the next afternoon.

    For most of the project the lag was a fixed three days, measured in August. When the feed sped up, that rule kept throwing away readings the job already had. On 2 November the forecast for the 3rd was built from 30 October's 11.4 µg/m³ while 31 October (79) and 1 November (106) were already in daily.csv. On 30 October no forecast went out at all: the one day the rule allowed was thin, and the days behind it had never been published.

    So the age of the reading is no longer assumed. It is counted from the target day, so a reading from D-2 is two days old. That is the youngest a day-ahead forecast can have, because the issue day is not over when the job runs. Each morning the job finds the freshest valid reading on file (features.freshest_age) and forecasts with a model fitted on readings of exactly that age (features.build(age=...)). A model fitted on two-day-old readings has learnt how much a two-day-old reading is worth, and it is never handed a five-day-old one. Past config.OBS_MAX_AGE, seven days, there is no forecast.

    The headline backtest is run at config.OBS_REFERENCE_AGE, two days, which is what the feed delivers now. The model lab scores the other ages as well, so a slower feed shows up as a measured cost.

    This matters more than it sounds, because it changes what "persistence" means.

    Persistence, twice¶

    Persistence normally means tomorrow equals today. That is the number in the proposal: MAE 6.5 µg/m³ over 89 day-pairs. It is a fair textbook baseline and it is what the marks were promised against.

    It is also not available to us. Predicting D from D-1 requires knowing D-1, and at issue time we do not. So the backtest reports both:

    • persistence — obs on D-1. The textbook bar, and the one the proposal targets. Uses information the model cannot have.
    • persistence_operational — the reading of the same age the model gets, two days old at the reference age. The same information set the model has.

    The first is the honest target. The second is the honest comparison. Reporting only one of them would be a way of choosing the answer in advance, so both go in data/metrics.json.

    Climatology and raw CAMS are carried alongside as sanity checks. Climatology is computed causally — the mean of everything observed strictly before the day in question, not the mean of the whole record, which would leak.

    Backtest¶

    code/evaluate.py walks forward one day at a time. For each target day it fits on every usable row strictly before that day, predicts one value, and moves on. No shuffled split, because the rows are a time series and shuffling lets the model see its own future.

    The ridge penalty alpha is chosen inside each fit, on the most recent fifth of that fit's training window. Choosing it once on the whole record would leak the test period into a hyperparameter — a small leak, but the kind that quietly inflates every number downstream.

    The first MIN_TRAIN = 40 rows are used for fitting only, never scored. Below that the fit is noise.

    Reported per run: MAE, RMSE, bias, correlation, and the rate at which the prediction lands in the correct CPCB National AQI band. The last one matters because a person reading a forecast wants to know whether tomorrow is Satisfactory or Poor, not whether the error was 7.2 or 7.4.

    Scores are split into calm and burning seasons, since the targets differ and the two regimes barely resemble each other.

    Why ridge, and why by hand¶

    The correction is linear in the features, with an L2 penalty, fitted through the normal equations and solved by Gaussian elimination. Roughly eighty lines in code/model.py.

    scikit-learn would be two lines. It would also put numpy, scipy and a compiled BLAS in the path of a job that has to run unattended every morning until November. The model is small enough that writing it out keeps the whole pipeline standard-library only, and a fitted model is then just the numbers in data/model.json — reproducible without installing anything.

    The features are few and mostly logged, so a linear model is not obviously the wrong shape. The model lab, below, now tests that directly: on this much data a random forest, gradient boosting and a small neural network all score worse than the ridge fit.

    The week 5 review¶

    The first fitted model lost to textbook persistence by 22.6% and, worse, had started drifting downward while the station was climbing. Pulling it apart found five separate faults, and the current model is what came out of fixing them.

    • The meteorology was never switched on. The backfill had been run weeks earlier and the fit was still reading CAMS, lagged observations and a seasonal term. Wind and fire counts, the two things most likely to matter in October, had never been tested.
    • doy_sin and doy_cos were not measuring seasonality. With four months of data a day-of-year term cannot tell a season from a trend, and these had learnt "PM2.5 falls through the monsoon". They carried almost as much weight as the CAMS forecast itself and would have kept extrapolating that decline into November. Dropped.
    • obs_recent and obs_recent_log were the same signal twice, split across two coefficients that pulled against each other. One kept.
    • obs_age had been constant at 4.0 since the feed was repaired, so it contributed nothing but still cost a parameter. It is still published with each forecast. Since November it picks which model forecasts instead of being fitted on, as described above.
    • Thin days were being trained on. CPCB does not consider a 24-hour mean valid below 16 hourly readings, and neither should the model. Days under that line are recorded and displayed but are no longer used as labels, nor fed forward as a later day's persistence.

    Two further changes did more than any of the above.

    Fitting in log space. PM2.5 spans an order of magnitude between a clean day and a burning-season one. On the raw scale the squared error is decided by the worst few days, and the fit bends towards them at the expense of the ordinary ones. log1p makes the penalty proportional, which is also how the error is felt: ten out on a reading of twenty matters, ten out on two hundred does not.

    Lagging the fire count. FIRMS records detections against the day they happened, so a target day's own count does not exist when its forecast goes out. It was a leak. Fixing it improved the model, because the physically correct signal was the lagged one all along — smoke lifted off a field upwind takes the better part of a day to arrive.

    A year and a half of history¶

    Everything above was first fitted on about a hundred days, all of them summer and monsoon. The table began on 15 May 2026 because the bootstrap asked Open-Meteo for past_days=92, on the belief that this was as far back as the CAMS archive went. It was only the ceiling on that one parameter. The live sensor has reported since February 2025, so the 2025 burning season had been available from the start.

    It mattered in October. On 3 and 5 October the live forecast was 22 µg/m³ against an observed 38: the model had never seen the season begin. scripts/extend_history.py pulled the table back to 19 February 2025, and every day of October and November 2025 came with it. Refitted on that history, the backtest gives 33 and 42 for the same two days. The data page has the details.

    With a burning season in the table, four ways of letting the season change the model were tested against the current one by scripts/compare_models.py: interactions on fire count and eastward wind, on persistence and CAMS, all four, and a separate burning-season fit. The bar was fixed first: clearly better in burning season, no more than a point or two worse in calm. None cleared it, so the model is unchanged.

    The model lab¶

    code/lab.py puts the shipped model next to every alternative worth trying, on the same days and with the same information.

    • Candidates. Three baselines (persistence, climatology, raw CAMS), then CAMS rescaled by its recent ratio to the station, the shipped ridge, ridge with the smoke terms from week 7, ridge corrected by half its mean error over the last seven published days, analog days (the average of the most similar past days), a random forest, gradient boosting, a small neural network, and an ensemble. The first eight need nothing outside the standard library and could ship. The scikit-learn models are benchmarks, installed for the lab alone (requirements-lab.txt).
    • Same rules for all. Every candidate is walked forward over the same days, from the same reading, and trains only on days whose readings had been published (evaluate.published). Anything that could ship is refitted daily, as the daily job would run it. The benchmarks are refitted weekly, because a daily refit of a forest takes minutes on its own.
    • Every reading age. The shipped model is also scored at ages two to seven days, which is how the cost of a slow feed is measured instead of guessed.
    • Shadow forecasts. Every morning each candidate issues its own forecast for tomorrow, frozen in data/shadow.json and scored when the observation lands. That builds a live record for each one, which no backtest can stand in for.

    A challenger replaces the shipped model only if it passes all of the rule in lab.PROMOTION. It must be at least 3 points of skill better in burning season and no more than 2 points worse in calm season. Those two thresholds were written down in week 7. It must stay within 5% of the shipped model's error at every reading age from two to five days. And it must do no worse over 20 live days. Promotion is then a one-line change to config.SHIPPED_EXTRA in a pull request. Nothing in the lab changes what ships.

    The age test was added for a reason. Ridge with the smoke terms clears the first two thresholds at two-day-old readings, but with three- and four-day-old readings it breaks down early in the 2025 season. With only a handful of burning days to learn from, the penalty chosen on a validation tail that contained none of them came out near zero, and one forecast for a day that measured 13 came out at 2684. A rule that only looked at one age would have shipped it.

    The likely range¶

    Each forecast comes with a range meant to hold the measured value four days in five. It used to be built from the spread of the fit's own errors, which is narrower than the real spread by construction. It held 15 of the first 27 live days.

    It is now the middle 80% of the backtest's out-of-sample errors, taken in log space and kept separate for each season (evaluate.calibrate). Under a log target the range comes out lopsided, wider above the forecast than below, which is the right shape for a quantity that spikes upward. Its honesty is checked the same way it is used (evaluate.coverage): each day's range is sized only from the days before it.

    The outlook¶

    Days two and three are forecast from the same station reading as tomorrow, so the reading is a day or two older for them, and each horizon gets a model fitted at that age. Their CAMS and weather come from the same morning's request and are kept in data/outlook.json rather than daily.csv, which only ever holds a day's day-ahead forecast. In the backtest the table has only day-ahead CAMS and weather for every horizon, which flatters days two and three a little; their live records are the check.

    Where it stands¶

    Results is regenerated every morning and is the current evaluation, split by season, by reading age and by model, with a live-only line.

    As of late November 2026, against persistence given the same two-day-old reading, the calm-season target is met (+13%) and burning season is short of its 20% (about +9%): the smoky first half of November, which the model under-forecast day after day, pulled it down. With readings four days old, the situation the proposal was written for, both are met (+22% in each season). The shipped ridge and its smoke-term variant have the lowest errors of any single models on the leaderboard, ahead of the forest, boosting and the network. The model still does not see spikes coming. The analog list on the dashboard helps there: when tomorrow's inputs sit closest to past days that turned out Poor, a lower forecast deserves a second look.
